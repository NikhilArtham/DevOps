## 📅 DAY 1 — Process Management

<details><summary>1. What is a process?</summary>A running instance of a program managed by the operating system.</details>
<details><summary>2. What is a PID?</summary>Process ID; it uniquely identifies a running process.</details>
<details><summary>3. What is a PPID?</summary>Parent Process ID; it identifies the parent process.</details>
<details><summary>4. ps vs top?</summary>`ps` gives a point-in-time snapshot; `top` continuously monitors processes and resources.</details>
<details><summary>5. top vs htop?</summary>Both monitor processes; `htop` provides a more interactive visual interface.</details>
<details><summary>6. What does ps aux show?</summary>Processes from all users in user-oriented format, including processes without a controlling terminal.</details>
<details><summary>7. What does ps -ef show?</summary>A full-format process list useful for PID, PPID, user and complete command information.</details>
<details><summary>8. How do you find a process?</summary>Use `pgrep`, `ps aux | grep`, or `ps -ef | grep`.</details>
<details><summary>9. Common process states?</summary>R running/runnable, S sleeping, D uninterruptible sleep, T stopped, Z zombie.</details>
<details><summary>10. What is a zombie?</summary>A process that finished execution but remains in the process table because its parent has not collected its status.</details>
<details><summary>11. What is a signal?</summary>A notification sent to a process requesting or indicating an action/event.</details>
<details><summary>12. What does kill PID do?</summary>It normally sends SIGTERM (15), requesting graceful termination.</details>
<details><summary>13. What does kill -9 do?</summary>It sends SIGKILL and forcefully terminates the process.</details>
<details><summary>14. SIGTERM vs SIGKILL?</summary>SIGTERM can be handled for cleanup; SIGKILL cannot be caught or handled.</details>
<details><summary>15. What is SIGINT?</summary>Signal 2, commonly generated by Ctrl+C.</details>
<details><summary>16. SIGSTOP vs SIGCONT?</summary>SIGSTOP pauses a process; SIGCONT resumes it.</details>
<details><summary>17. What is SIGHUP?</summary>Historically hangup; many daemons use it to request configuration reload, depending on the application.</details>
<details><summary>18. kill -15 vs kill -9?</summary>`kill -15` sends graceful SIGTERM; `kill -9` sends forceful SIGKILL.</details>
<details><summary>19. How do you troubleshoot high CPU?</summary>Use `top/htop`, identify the PID, inspect with `ps`, correlate with logs/application behavior, then remediate appropriately.</details>
<details><summary>20. Why is process management important?</summary>It is fundamental to troubleshooting CPU, memory, hung processes, failed services, crashes and production incidents.</details>

## 📅 DAY 2 — Filesystems, Mounts, Inodes & Disk Troubleshooting

<details><summary>1. What is a filesystem?</summary>The structure/rules Linux uses to store and organize files and metadata.</details>
<details><summary>2. What is a mount point?</summary>A directory where a filesystem is attached to the Linux directory tree.</details>
<details><summary>3. What does df -h show?</summary>Filesystem-level disk capacity and usage in human-readable units.</details>
<details><summary>4. df vs du?</summary>`df` reports filesystem usage; `du` investigates directory/file usage.</details>
<details><summary>5. How do you check inode usage?</summary>`df -i` or `df -ih`.</details>
<details><summary>6. Can free disk space coexist with inability to create files?</summary>Yes. The filesystem may have exhausted its inodes.</details>
<details><summary>7. What is an inode?</summary>Filesystem metadata containing ownership, permissions, timestamps, size and references to data blocks.</details>
<details><summary>8. What does lsblk -f show?</summary>Block devices plus filesystem type, UUID, labels and mount points.</details>
<details><summary>9. What does findmnt do?</summary>Shows structured information about mounted filesystems.</details>
<details><summary>10. How do you mount a filesystem?</summary>`mount <device> <mount-point>`.</details>
<details><summary>11. What is /etc/fstab?</summary>Persistent filesystem mount configuration used during boot or `mount -a`.</details>
<details><summary>12. Why is bad fstab dangerous?</summary>Incorrect entries can cause mount, service or boot problems.</details>
<details><summary>13. How do you find large directories?</summary>Use targeted `du`, for example `du -xh --max-depth=1 /var | sort -hr`.</details>
<details><summary>14. How do you find large files?</summary>Use targeted `find`/`du` output and sort it by size.</details>
<details><summary>15. What does find -xdev do?</summary>It prevents `find` from crossing into other mounted filesystems.</details>
<details><summary>16. Why can df be full while du does not explain it?</summary>A deleted file may still be open by a process; use `lsof +L1`.</details>
<details><summary>17. Common causes of inode exhaustion?</summary>Millions of small files, temporary files, caches, sessions, log fragments and application bugs.</details>
<details><summary>18. Production disk troubleshooting flow?</summary>Check `df -h/-i`, inspect storage/mounts, drill with `du`, find large files, identify the owner, clean safely and prevent recurrence.</details>
<details><summary>19. Why avoid unrestricted du /?</summary>It can scan huge numbers of files and cross other filesystems.</details>
<details><summary>20. Why not immediately rm large files?</summary>First establish ownership, purpose, retention requirements and active use.</details>

## 📅 DAY 3 — Permissions, Ownership, ACLs & sudo

<details><summary>1. What are Linux permissions?</summary>Read, write and execute controls for owner, group and others.</details>
<details><summary>2. What do u, g and o mean?</summary>`u` owner/user, `g` group, `o` others.</details>
<details><summary>3. What does chmod 755 mean?</summary>Owner rwx; group and others r-x.</details>
<details><summary>4. chmod +x vs chmod 755?</summary>`+x` adds execute without resetting other permissions; `755` explicitly sets the mode.</details>
<details><summary>5. What does chmod -R mean?</summary>Apply the permission operation recursively.</details>
<details><summary>6. What does chown do?</summary>Changes file ownership.</details>
<details><summary>7. chown user vs user:group?</summary>The first changes owner; the second changes both owner and group.</details>
<details><summary>8. What is an ACL?</summary>Granular per-user/per-group access beyond basic owner/group/other permissions.</details>
<details><summary>9. What does setfacl -m mean?</summary>Modify/add an ACL entry.</details>
<details><summary>10. What does setfacl -x do?</summary>Remove the specified ACL entry.</details>
<details><summary>11. What does setfacl -b do?</summary>Remove all extended ACL entries.</details>
<details><summary>12. What is a default ACL?</summary>A directory ACL inherited by newly created files/directories.</details>
<details><summary>13. What is the ACL mask?</summary>It limits effective permissions of named users/groups and the owning group.</details>
<details><summary>14. How do you inspect ACLs?</summary>`getfacl <file-or-directory>`.</details>
<details><summary>15. What is sudo?</summary>A mechanism allowing authorized users to execute commands with another user's privileges.</details>
<details><summary>16. What does sudo -l show?</summary>The current user's permitted sudo commands.</details>
<details><summary>17. Why use visudo?</summary>It validates sudoers syntax before saving, reducing the risk of breaking sudo.</details>
<details><summary>18. How troubleshoot Permission denied?</summary>Check identity, path traversal with `namei`, ownership/mode bits, ACLs and sudo policy.</details>
<details><summary>19. Linux permissions vs AWS IAM?</summary>Linux permissions control OS resources; IAM controls AWS API/resource authorization.</details>
<details><summary>20. Safest production permission fix?</summary>Identify the exact failing identity/resource, make the smallest intended change, verify it and avoid broad permissions.</details>

## 📅 DAY 4 — systemd Services, Dependencies & Restart Behavior

<details><summary>1. What is systemd?</summary>The Linux system and service manager.</details>
<details><summary>2. What is systemctl?</summary>The CLI used to manage systemd units.</details>
<details><summary>3. start vs enable?</summary>`start` runs now; `enable` configures automatic startup.</details>
<details><summary>4. What does enable --now do?</summary>Enables the unit and starts it immediately.</details>
<details><summary>5. Active vs enabled?</summary>Active is runtime state; enabled is startup configuration.</details>
<details><summary>6. How troubleshoot a failed service?</summary>Use `systemctl status`, `journalctl -u`, then inspect exit code, configuration, user, permissions, dependencies and application behavior.</details>
<details><summary>7. What is journalctl?</summary>The CLI for querying the systemd journal.</details>
<details><summary>8. How follow service logs?</summary>`journalctl -u service -f`.</details>
<details><summary>9. What is daemon-reload?</summary>Reloads changed systemd unit definitions.</details>
<details><summary>10. Requires vs Wants?</summary>`Requires` is stronger; `Wants` is a weaker desired dependency.</details>
<details><summary>11. Does After= create a dependency?</summary>No. It controls ordering when both units are involved.</details>
<details><summary>12. Restart=on-failure?</summary>Restarts the service after a failure.</details>
<details><summary>13. Why use RestartSec?</summary>To delay restart attempts and reduce rapid crash loops.</details>
<details><summary>14. Why might a service immediately exit?</summary>Bad configuration, missing files, permissions, ports, dependencies, environment or application crashes.</details>
<details><summary>15. How find the service user?</summary>`systemctl show service -p User -p Group`.</details>
<details><summary>16. How inspect a unit?</summary>`systemctl cat service` and `systemctl show service`.</details>
<details><summary>17. Missing ExecStart executable?</summary>The service fails and the reason appears in status/journal.</details>
<details><summary>18. Why repeated restarts?</summary>Usually an underlying application/configuration/dependency failure; inspect logs before changing restart policy.</details>
<details><summary>19. When is daemon-reload required?</summary>After changing a unit file.</details>
<details><summary>20. Is every service failure a systemd failure?</summary>No. systemd may correctly report an application, permission, network or dependency failure.</details>

## 📅 DAY 5 — journalctl & Log-Driven Troubleshooting

<details><summary>1. What is journalctl?</summary>The command-line tool for querying systemd journal logs.</details>
<details><summary>2. What is journald?</summary>The systemd logging service that collects journal entries.</details>
<details><summary>3. What does -u mean?</summary>Filter by systemd unit/service.</details>
<details><summary>4. What does -f mean?</summary>Follow new log entries in real time.</details>
<details><summary>5. What does -n mean?</summary>Limit output to a number of recent entries.</details>
<details><summary>6. What does -b mean?</summary>Filter logs by boot; `-b -1` targets the previous boot.</details>
<details><summary>7. What does -p mean?</summary>Filter by log priority.</details>
<details><summary>8. How filter by time?</summary>Use `--since` and `--until`.</details>
<details><summary>9. What does -k show?</summary>Kernel messages.</details>
<details><summary>10. What does --no-pager do?</summary>Prints directly instead of opening a pager.</details>
<details><summary>11. What does -g do?</summary>Filters messages by a pattern.</details>
<details><summary>12. How inspect failed-service logs?</summary>`systemctl status service` then `journalctl -u service`.</details>
<details><summary>13. Why find the first meaningful error?</summary>Later messages may be consequences; the first relevant failure is often the root cause.</details>
<details><summary>14. How troubleshoot repeated restarts?</summary>Inspect status, journal, exit codes, restart configuration and the underlying application.</details>
<details><summary>15. How check journal disk usage?</summary>`journalctl --disk-usage`.</details>
<details><summary>16. How vacuum by age?</summary>`journalctl --vacuum-time=7d`.</details>
<details><summary>17. Why use time filtering?</summary>It narrows the incident window and reduces noise.</details>
<details><summary>18. Why correlate logs with other evidence?</summary>Logs provide clues; metrics, sockets, configuration and dependency checks validate the hypothesis.</details>
<details><summary>19. How investigate Connection refused?</summary>Check the target listener, service status and network path rather than blindly restarting the client.</details>
<details><summary>20. What is the DevOps log-driven method?</summary>Symptom → component → time → logs → first useful error → correlation → verification → root-cause fix → verification.</details>

## 📅 DAY 6 — grep, sed, awk, sort & xargs

<details><summary>1. What is grep?</summary>A text search/filtering command.</details>
<details><summary>2. What does grep -i do?</summary>Ignores case.</details>
<details><summary>3. What does grep -n do?</summary>Shows matching line numbers.</details>
<details><summary>4. What does grep -v do?</summary>Inverts the match.</details>
<details><summary>5. What does grep -E do?</summary>Uses extended regular expressions.</details>
<details><summary>6. What is sed?</summary>A stream editor used for substitution, selection and deletion of text.</details>
<details><summary>7. What does sed s/old/new/g mean?</summary>Substitute `old` with `new` for all matches on each line.</details>
<details><summary>8. Why be careful with sed -i?</summary>It modifies the actual file.</details>
<details><summary>9. What is awk?</summary>A text-processing tool especially useful for fields, filtering and calculations.</details>
<details><summary>10. What are $0 and $1 in awk?</summary>`$0` is the whole line; `$1` is the first field.</details>
<details><summary>11. What does awk -F do?</summary>Sets the field separator.</details>
<details><summary>12. What does $NF mean?</summary>The last field in the current awk record.</details>
<details><summary>13. What does sort -n do?</summary>Numeric sorting.</details>
<details><summary>14. What does sort -h do?</summary>Human-readable numeric sorting such as K/M/G.</details>
<details><summary>15. What does sort -k do?</summary>Sort by a specific field/key.</details>
<details><summary>16. What is xargs?</summary>Builds command arguments from standard input.</details>
<details><summary>17. Why use find -print0 | xargs -0?</summary>It safely handles filenames containing spaces and special characters.</details>
<details><summary>18. What is a high-value log pipeline?</summary>For example `awk` to extract fields, `sort`, `uniq -c`, then `sort -nr` to rank results.</details>
<details><summary>19. Why inspect before using xargs with destructive commands?</summary>Incorrect input can cause unintended operations.</details>
<details><summary>20. Why are these commands important in DevOps?</summary>They turn large logs and command output into evidence during production troubleshooting.</details>

## 📅 DAY 7 — Bash Scripting

<details><summary>1. What is a Bash variable?</summary>A named shell value assigned with `name=value`.</details>
<details><summary>2. Why quote variables?</summary>To preserve argument boundaries and reduce word-splitting/special-character problems.</details>
<details><summary>3. What is command substitution?</summary>`$(command)` runs a command and substitutes its output.</details>
<details><summary>4. What is `$?`?</summary>It contains the previous command's exit status.</details>
<details><summary>5. What does exit 0 mean?</summary>Successful completion by convention.</details>
<details><summary>6. return vs exit?</summary>`return` exits a function; `exit` terminates the script.</details>
<details><summary>7. What is $1?</summary>The first positional argument.</details>
<details><summary>8. What is "$@"?</summary>All positional arguments while preserving each argument boundary.</details>
<details><summary>9. What is export?</summary>It makes a variable available to child processes.</details>
<details><summary>10. for vs while?</summary>`for` iterates over items/counters; `while` repeats while a condition remains true.</details>
<details><summary>11. What is a Bash function?</summary>A reusable block of shell logic that can accept arguments and return a status.</details>
<details><summary>12. Why use local?</summary>To scope a function variable and avoid unintended changes outside the function.</details>
<details><summary>13. What does set -euo pipefail mean?</summary>Failure handling, unset-variable protection and pipeline failure propagation.</details>
<details><summary>14. Is set -e a universal error handler?</summary>No. Bash has contexts where `set -e` does not behave like a global exception handler.</details>
<details><summary>15. && vs ;?</summary>`&&` requires previous success; `;` runs the next command regardless of previous status.</details>
<details><summary>16. How check Bash syntax?</summary>`bash -n script.sh`.</details>
<details><summary>17. How trace a script?</summary>`bash -x script.sh`.</details>
<details><summary>18. Why are exit codes important in CI/CD?</summary>Automation uses them to decide whether a step succeeded or failed.</details>
<details><summary>19. What makes production Bash reliable?</summary>Validation, quoting, explicit checks, safe error handling, useful logs and accurate exit codes.</details>
<details><summary>20. What is the danger of assuming manual and automated execution have the same environment?</summary>Schedulers and CI runners may use different users, PATHs, working directories, variables and credentials.</details>

## 📅 DAY 8 — Bash Error Handling, Traps & Safe Automation

<details><summary>1. What is trap?</summary>It registers commands/functions for signals or shell events.</details>
<details><summary>2. What is trap EXIT used for?</summary>Cleanup on script exit, such as removing temporary files.</details>
<details><summary>3. What is trap ERR?</summary>A handler for eligible command failures; it has Bash-specific exceptions.</details>
<details><summary>4. What is SIGTERM?</summary>A termination request that can be handled for graceful cleanup.</details>
<details><summary>5. SIGTERM vs SIGKILL?</summary>SIGTERM can be handled; SIGKILL cannot and forcefully terminates the process.</details>
<details><summary>6. Why use mktemp?</summary>To create unpredictable temporary files/directories safely.</details>
<details><summary>7. Why use cleanup traps?</summary>To remove temporary resources on normal and many failure exits.</details>
<details><summary>8. What does set -u protect against?</summary>Unset variables are treated as errors.</details>
<details><summary>9. Why is set -e not enough?</summary>It has Bash-specific contexts and is not a universal exception mechanism.</details>
<details><summary>10. What is pipefail?</summary>It makes pipeline status reflect earlier command failures.</details>
<details><summary>11. Why validate arguments?</summary>To prevent missing/unsafe input from causing incorrect or destructive automation.</details>
<details><summary>12. Why use flock?</summary>To prevent multiple instances of the same automation from running concurrently.</details>
<details><summary>13. Why is a lock file alone unreliable?</summary>A stale file can remain after a crash; `flock` represents an actual held lock.</details>
<details><summary>14. How log the failing line?</summary>Use an ERR trap with `$LINENO` and `$BASH_COMMAND`.</details>
<details><summary>15. Deployment fails after stopping the service. What should you consider?</summary>Cleanup, recovery, rollback and whether validation could happen before stopping the live service.</details>
<details><summary>16. How handle AWS CLI failures?</summary>Check the command status, log the failure, stop unsafe follow-up work and return non-zero.</details>
<details><summary>17. How do traps help long-running jobs?</summary>They allow graceful signal handling and cleanup.</details>
<details><summary>18. What makes Bash automation production-safe?</summary>Validation, quoting, explicit error handling, cleanup, locking where needed, logging, recovery planning and accurate exit codes.</details>
<details><summary>19. Why should cleanup be idempotent?</summary>It may run during multiple exit paths, so repeating cleanup should not create a new failure.</details>
<details><summary>20. What should a deployment script do after a failed change?</summary>Stop unsafe follow-up actions, preserve useful evidence, return a failure status and invoke the approved recovery/rollback path.</details>

## 📅 DAY 9 — Cron, systemd Timers & Scheduled Automation

<details><summary>1. What is cron?</summary>A traditional Unix/Linux scheduler for recurring jobs.</details>
<details><summary>2. How edit a user crontab?</summary>`crontab -e`.</details>
<details><summary>3. How list a crontab?</summary>`crontab -l`.</details>
<details><summary>4. What does crontab -r do?</summary>Removes the current user's entire crontab.</details>
<details><summary>5. What are cron's five fields?</summary>Minute, hour, day-of-month, month, day-of-week.</details>
<details><summary>6. Why can cron jobs work manually but fail under cron?</summary>The environment differs: PATH, working directory, variables, user and terminal.</details>
<details><summary>7. Why use absolute paths in cron?</summary>To avoid PATH and working-directory assumptions.</details>
<details><summary>8. How capture cron output?</summary>Redirect stdout/stderr, e.g. `>> /var/log/job.log 2>&1`.</details>
<details><summary>9. Cron vs systemd timers?</summary>Cron is simple/portable; systemd timers integrate with services, dependencies and journald.</details>
<details><summary>10. What does a systemd timer trigger?</summary>Normally a corresponding `.service` unit.</details>
<details><summary>11. What is Type=oneshot?</summary>A service type intended for work that runs and exits.</details>
<details><summary>12. What does OnCalendar do?</summary>Defines a calendar/time-based schedule.</details>
<details><summary>13. What does Persistent=true do?</summary>Allows a missed calendar event to be handled after the timer becomes active again.</details>
<details><summary>14. OnBootSec vs OnUnitActiveSec?</summary>They schedule relative to boot versus relative to the last activation of the associated unit.</details>
<details><summary>15. How inspect timers?</summary>`systemctl list-timers --all` and `systemctl status timer`.</details>
<details><summary>16. Timer triggers but job fails. What do you inspect?</summary>The `.service` status and `journalctl -u service`.</details>
<details><summary>17. How prevent overlapping scheduled jobs?</summary>Use mechanisms such as `flock` and design the job to be idempotent where possible.</details>
<details><summary>18. What is the scheduled-automation troubleshooting chain?</summary>Scheduler → trigger → environment → permissions → command → exit status → expected result.</details>
<details><summary>19. Why can a timer be active while the job is failing?</summary>The timer and service are separate units; the timer can successfully trigger a service that then exits unsuccessfully.</details>
<details><summary>20. What evidence proves a scheduled job failed?</summary>The scheduler/timer state plus the service exit state and service journal for the relevant execution window.</details>

## 📅 DAY 10 — SSH: Keys, Agent, Tunneling & Common Failures

<details><summary>1. What is SSH?</summary>SSH is an encrypted protocol for secure remote login and command execution.</details>
<details><summary>2. Public key vs private key?</summary>The public key is authorized on the server; the private key remains secret on the client.</details>
<details><summary>3. Where is an SSH public key normally authorized?</summary>In the remote user's `~/.ssh/authorized_keys`.</details>
<details><summary>4. Why use chmod 600 for a private key?</summary>It prevents group and other users from reading the private key.</details>
<details><summary>5. What does ssh -vvv do?</summary>It provides detailed SSH client-side debugging.</details>
<details><summary>6. What is ssh-agent?</summary>A process that holds identities in memory for SSH authentication.</details>
<details><summary>7. What does ssh-add -l do?</summary>Lists fingerprints of identities loaded into the agent.</details>
<details><summary>8. What is agent forwarding?</summary>It makes the client's SSH agent available through an SSH session to an intermediate host.</details>
<details><summary>9. What is the security risk of agent forwarding?</summary>A compromised intermediate host may be able to use the forwarded agent while the session is active.</details>
<details><summary>10. What is ProxyJump?</summary>`ssh -J` routes an SSH connection through a bastion/jump host.</details>
<details><summary>11. ProxyJump vs agent forwarding?</summary>ProxyJump provides the network path through a bastion; agent forwarding provides access to the client's authentication agent.</details>
<details><summary>12. What does -L mean?</summary>Local port forwarding: local port → SSH server → destination.</details>
<details><summary>13. What does -R mean?</summary>Remote port forwarding: a remote listening port forwards through SSH to a client-side destination.</details>
<details><summary>14. What does -D mean?</summary>Dynamic SOCKS proxy forwarding.</details>
<details><summary>15. SSH timeout vs publickey failure?</summary>A timeout is usually reachability/network/service-layer; `Permission denied (publickey)` indicates authentication/configuration after SSH is reached.</details>
<details><summary>16. How troubleshoot too many authentication failures?</summary>Inspect `ssh-add -l` and use `IdentitiesOnly=yes` with the intended key.</details>
<details><summary>17. What does a changed host-key warning mean?</summary>The known host key differs; verify rebuild/rotation/IP change before changing `known_hosts` because interception is also possible.</details>
<details><summary>18. What should you check when a tunnel fails?</summary>SSH connectivity, local listener, destination reachability from the SSH server, destination service and security controls.</details>
<details><summary>19. SSH vs AWS IAM?</summary>IAM authorizes AWS API actions; SSH authenticates and authorizes access to the Linux OS.</details>
<details><summary>20. What is the SSH troubleshooting mental model?</summary>`REACH → LISTEN → AUTHENTICATE → AUTHORIZE → SESSION → APPLICATION`; classify the failing layer before changing configuration.</details>

## 📅 DAY 11 — October Production Troubleshooting + Monthly Mock Interview

<details><summary>1. A Linux service is active but users cannot connect. What do you check?</summary>Do not equate active with reachable. Check the service status and logs, verify the expected listening socket with `ss -lntp`, confirm the bind address and port, then check host/network controls and dependencies. Identify the first failed layer before changing anything.</details>
<details><summary>2. What is the difference between df and du, including the deleted-open-file case?</summary>`df` reports filesystem-level allocated/used space; `du` calculates usage from directory entries. If a large file is deleted while a process still has it open, `df` can remain high while `du` no longer shows that file. `lsof +L1` can identify such files.</details>
<details><summary>3. A process consumes 100% CPU. How do you investigate safely?</summary>Identify the PID with `top`/`ps`, inspect command/user/state, correlate the timing with application logs and recent changes, determine whether the load is expected, then remediate according to the application runbook. Avoid `kill -9` as the first response because it skips graceful cleanup.</details>
<details><summary>4. Why can a systemd service fail after a successful deployment?</summary>The deployment can succeed while runtime configuration fails: missing environment variables, wrong permissions, missing files, a port conflict, dependency failure, bad ExecStart or an application startup error. `systemctl status`, `systemctl cat` and `journalctl -u` provide evidence.</details>
<details><summary>5. Why can cron work manually but fail when scheduled?</summary>Cron can run with a different user, PATH, working directory, environment variables, credentials and shell context. Use absolute paths, explicit environment/configuration, correct permissions and captured stdout/stderr.</details>
<details><summary>6. An EC2 instance cannot reach the internet. What is your investigation order?</summary>Identify the instance/subnet, inspect the associated route table, verify the expected IGW or NAT path, then inspect NACL/Security Group controls, DNS and the destination. For private instances, confirm the default route targets a working NAT Gateway when public internet access is required.</details>
<details><summary>7. An ALB returns 503. What does that tell you?</summary>It does not automatically mean the server is down. Investigate listener/rule selection, target-group membership and target health, health-check path/port, target SG reachability and whether the application is actually listening/responding. An unhealthy target set is a common cause.</details>
<details><summary>8. An application gets S3 AccessDenied. How do you isolate the policy layer?</summary>Identify the caller with STS, then inspect identity policy, bucket/resource policy, explicit denies, SCPs, permissions boundaries and session policies. Verify the bucket/object ARN and policy conditions. If SSE-KMS is involved, separately verify KMS authorization.</details>
<details><summary>9. EC2 cannot connect to RDS on 5432. What evidence separates DNS, network and authentication?</summary>`dig`/`getent hosts` tests name resolution. `nc -vz <endpoint> 5432` tests TCP reachability. If TCP succeeds but the database rejects credentials, the network path is working and the investigation moves to authentication/authorization. A TCP timeout points back toward routing or network controls.</details>
<details><summary>10. How do you prove an AWS change caused an incident?</summary>Use CloudWatch to establish the behavioral timeline—metrics, alarms, target health and errors—then use CloudTrail to identify AWS API activity, principal, resource and request parameters around the same time. Correlate the change with the first symptom and validate that the changed configuration explains the observed failure. Time correlation alone is not proof.</details>
<details><summary>11. Users report intermittent failures. What do you establish first?</summary>Establish scope, affected resources/users, exact start and end times, error patterns, frequency, dependency impact and recent changes. Then compare metrics and logs across the incident window instead of restarting components without evidence.</details>
<details><summary>12. What makes a troubleshooting hypothesis strong?</summary>It explains the observed symptom, predicts evidence that should exist if the hypothesis is true, can be tested without unnecessary risk and can be falsified. A good hypothesis narrows the investigation rather than creating more guesses.</details>
<details><summary>13. Is a recent configuration change automatically the root cause?</summary>No. A recent change is strong temporal evidence but not proof. Confirm that the change affected the relevant resource, that the timing matches the symptom, and that the changed behavior explains the failure.</details>
<details><summary>14. Why avoid making many changes simultaneously during an incident?</summary>Multiple changes increase blast radius and can destroy causal evidence. If the incident improves, you may not know which change fixed it; if it worsens, rollback becomes harder. Prefer the smallest reversible change supported by evidence.</details>
<details><summary>15. What belongs in a final incident summary?</summary>Impact, detection, timeline, evidence, root cause, remediation, validation and prevention/follow-up actions. The summary should distinguish facts proven by evidence from assumptions or contributing factors.</details>
